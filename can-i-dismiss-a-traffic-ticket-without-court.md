# Can I Dismiss a Traffic Ticket Without Going to Court?

Yes, in most states. You can dismiss a traffic ticket without a court appearance by completing traffic school or defensive driving, by requesting a trial by written declaration in California, or by agreeing to deferred adjudication. Serious violations, CDL holders and some courts still require a personal appearance.

By the DismissTicket Team

Updated 2026-10-28

## The short answer

Most traffic tickets are infractions, and most states offer at least one administrative path that closes the case without a courtroom. The three paths below differ in who qualifies, what you pay, and how long the process takes. Each one ends with no points on your record when it is completed correctly.

You still have to act before your deadline. Most states give you 15 to 30 days from the date of the citation to respond, and a missed deadline can add fines, suspend your license or produce a bench warrant.

## Option 1: Traffic school or defensive driving

This is the most common no-court dismissal. You ask the court for permission, complete a state-approved course, and send the completion certificate back to the court. The case is then dismissed.

- **Time:** most approved courses take 4 to 8 hours, and you can split that across several sessions.
- **Cost:** course fees usually run $15 to $50. The court also charges an administrative fee that varies by state and county.
- **Records:** a dismissed ticket does not add points and normally does not appear on an insurance record pull. It can still be visible to law enforcement.
- **Limit:** most states allow this once every 12 to 18 months.

Traffic school also has eligibility limits. Excessive speed, a school zone violation and a commercial vehicle usually disqualify you. Check the state guide for your ticket before you pay a course fee.

## Option 2: Trial by written declaration (California)

California allows you to contest an infraction by mail under CVC 40902. You request form TR-205 from the court, pay the full bail amount, and submit a written defense. The officer submits a written statement too, and a judge rules by mail.

Two details matter. The bail is refundable if you win. If you lose, you can request a Trial de Novo, which is a new in-person hearing, within 20 days of the mailed verdict. Officers frequently fail to file their statement, and that alone results in a dismissal.

This path exists because it is a real trial on the record, not a request for leniency. It suits a driver who has evidence, such as a defective sign, an unclear marking or a calibration record.

## Option 3: Deferred adjudication or deferred disposition

Some states postpone the case instead of deciding it. You plead no contest, the court sets a probationary period that usually runs 90 to 180 days, and the ticket is dismissed if you stay violation-free. No conviction appears on your record.

Texas courts use this option often, and many other states offer a version of it under names such as deferred disposition or diversion. Ask the court clerk whether your ticket qualifies. You normally pay the fine plus a fee, and the dismissal is not automatic: you must complete the period without a new violation.

## When you cannot avoid a court appearance

Some cases need a judge, and some drivers cannot use the administrative paths at all.

- **CDL holders.** Federal rule 49 CFR 384.226 stops states from masking, deferring or diverting a conviction for a commercial driver. Traffic school and deferred adjudication are often unavailable.
- **Serious violations.** Reckless driving, speeds of 25 mph or more over the limit, and school zone violations are usually excluded from traffic school.
- **Courts that require appearance.** A small number of courts, often rural ones, require an in-person hearing even for a routine infraction.

In these cases, a traffic ticket attorney is worth the fee. A typical attorney charge is $150 to $400, which is often less than the three-year insurance increase from a single conviction.
